# Voice Register Standard

## Purpose

The Jack Mallory Series holds three tonal traditions inside one prose voice: the operational gravity of *Heat*, the ensemble warmth of *Leverage*, and the elite charisma of *White Collar* and *Suits*. These three do not naturally cohabit inside a single paragraph. The series manages them by scene register.

Every scene has one dominant register, decided immediately after POV. The dominant register drives paragraph shape, sentence length, dialogue density, interior-monologue budget, and the character who carries charm. But the register is *dominant,* not *exclusive.* Every scene must earn its humanity somewhere. A scene that obeys the register perfectly and fails to breathe is a failed scene.

**Every scene must contain at least one moment of human weight** — a small physical act of care, a memory that catches a character mid-sentence, a silence where two people see each other, a moment of unexpected warmth, sharpness, or vulnerability. In Register A that moment may be brief and stolen. In Register B it may be constant. In Register C it may be the beat that lifts the whole scene above transactional exchange. The scene decides where and how, but the moment must be there.

## Register A — Operational

**Reference:** *Heat*.
**Scene types:** access, entry, surveillance, extraction, physical danger, tactical planning under time pressure, live crisis.

### Signature

- Short simple declarative sentences dominate.
- Beat-paragraphs (single-sentence paragraphs) are expected and pace the reader through professional load.
- Interior monologue is near-zero. State is revealed through action, gesture, and physical detail.
- Numeric specificity is mandatory: times to the minute, distances to the foot, durations tracked.
- Geography is established before any action depends on it.
- Dialogue is spare, functional, and often cover-based. Silences carry weight.
- No literary flourish. No em dashes except for a genuine interruption. No semicolons.
- Fear, doubt, exhaustion, and injury are shown through the body, never named.

### Charm carrier

None. Register A is not a charm register. Any attempt at wit falls to zero.

### Failure modes

- Narrator explaining what the character is feeling.
- Sentences of uniform length.
- "The kind of…" or "not this, but that" constructions.
- Any interior epiphany.

## Register B — Domestic/Crew

**Reference:** *Leverage*.
**Scene types:** Lowell dinners, planning at the workshop, Anton's shop after hours, Jack visiting Stan, Reese at Nina's, recovery scenes, aftermath.

### Signature

- Longer paragraphs permitted. Beat-paragraphs restricted.
- Dialogue overlaps, interrupts, trails off. Untranslated in-jokes and shorthand are welcome.
- Physical warmth (food, hands on shoulders, shared chairs, a dog underfoot) rendered without commentary.
- Interior monologue moderate. Characters may notice each other, but the narrator does not annotate.
- Numeric specificity relaxed. Time is measured by meals, seasons, and how long ago something was.
- Humor lives here. It is dry, character-specific, and often protective.
- Silences are companionable, not tense.

### Charm carrier

Distributed. Nina carries technical/sarcastic charm. Reese carries kinetic/irreverent charm. Anton carries deadpan charm. Marcus carries dry paternal charm. Jack is quieter here than in Register C — he listens more than he performs. The crew's love for him is shown by how they treat him, not by anything he says.

### Failure modes

- Sitcom banter.
- Every character funny in the same way.
- Sentimentality on the page.
- Naming the family bond.

## Register C — Elite/Adversarial

**Reference:** *White Collar*, *Suits*.
**Scene types:** WNG offices, galas, fundraisers, executive dinners, Addison at work, Jack meeting a wealthy referral, Hattie in a formal interview, boardroom confrontations.

### Signature

- Sentences may be longer and more elegant, but never ornate.
- Dialogue is precise, cordial, subtext-loaded, and quotable. Every line should carry leverage or character.
- Institutional and aesthetic detail named specifically: the tailor, the vintage, the maker, the school, the case citation, the deal terminology.
- Interior monologue lean but present. The POV reads the room and its incentives.
- Charm carried by the person best fit to the room. In Jack's POV, Jack carries it. In an Addison POV, Addison carries it. In an Avery POV facing Weaver, Avery carries a quieter version of it.
- Emotion runs behind civility. What is not said is louder than what is said.

### Charm carrier

Jack in his own scenes. Addison in her own scenes. Nina if she is running the room as a false persona. Marcus if he is operating in executive-protection mode. Never everyone at once.

### Failure modes

- Charm that fills space rather than moving leverage.
- Elite detail used for decoration rather than character.
- Two characters equally charming in the same scene (charm should have an owner per beat).
- Suits-style monologue speeches.

## Transitions

A single chapter may transition between registers. Transitions happen at the paragraph break, never mid-paragraph.

Common transition patterns:

- **C → A**: an elite meeting escalates into an operational moment (Chapter 7, Tomas → Rylan observing). The sentence length shortens, interior thought drops, geography reasserts.
- **A → B**: an operation ends and the crew regathers (post-gala aftermath). The paragraphs lengthen, dialogue softens, humor returns cautiously.
- **B → A**: planning ends and execution begins. The workshop scene closes; the next paragraph is Nina at a screen or Reese on a wall.
- **B → C**: a crew member steps into a false persona for a room. Register shifts as they cross the threshold, not before.

A chapter should not oscillate more than twice between registers. Register whiplash reads as tonal instability.

## The three DNAs — concrete craft techniques

### Leverage warmth — the family that shows love sideways

Not: characters telling each other they matter. Not: warmth stated by the narrator. Not: sitcom banter.

Do:

- Small physical acts of care that go unremarked. Someone pours the second cup. Someone moves a chair closer to the window. Someone hands over a coat without breaking the conversation.
- In-jokes and shorthand that the reader is not walked through. Trust the reader to catch up.
- Food, drink, hands, shoulders — the small currency of family, rendered without narration.
- One character noticing another and doing something small in response. This should happen in every crew scene, at least once.
- Humor as protective texture. Dry. Character-specific. Never sitcom.

### Heat gravity — adults who see each other

Not: coldness. Not: absence of feeling. Not: refusal of interior.

Do:

- Long silences that carry weight. The city outside the window. Coffee going cold.
- Adult mutual recognition between characters, including adversaries. A moment where the case stops mattering and two people just look at each other.
- Consequences that persist. Injuries that stay. Damage that does not heal on the page.
- Weather and geography treated as real, not atmospheric.
- Restraint that reveals rather than suppresses. What is not said carries more than what is.

### White Collar / Suits charm — smart people meeting smart people

Not: filler wit. Not: everyone charming at once. Not: monologues.

Do:

- Precise, cordial dialogue that carries subtext. Every line does work for character or leverage.
- Institutional and aesthetic specificity: this vintage, this maker, this school, this case citation, this deal terminology. Named because the character notices, not because the narrator decorates.
- A line in every C scene that is quotable — a sentence the reader wants to write down.
- Wit under pressure. Elegance in dialogue even inside grief or danger.
- Aesthetic pleasure rendered credibly. The sensory richness of an elite room, felt by the POV who knows what it costs.
- Recognition between smart people, and the room registering it.

## Distributed charm doctrine

The series treats charm as a shared resource of the crew, not a fixed attribute of Jack. This decision is deliberate and load-bearing for Book One in particular, where Jack's arc is about *withholding.* A charm-forward Jack in every scene would undercut the fracture with Marcus and the failure at the gala.

Rules:

- Jack is devastating in Register C rooms where he is operating.
- Jack is quieter in Register B, where he listens, cooks, drives, or sits.
- Nina carries wit and technical fluency in Register B and in Register C when running a persona.
- Reese carries kinetic humor and irreverence in Register B and in Register A when reconnaissance goes right.
- Anton carries deadpan and terse warmth in Register B.
- Marcus carries dry paternal command in Register B and executive gravity in Register C.
- Addison carries predatory grace in her own Register C scenes; she is the only character whose charm is untrusted by the reader.
- Hattie does not carry charm. She carries professional weight.

No scene should have two crew members competing for the same charm register at once. If they are both funny, one of them is funny about the other.

## Register temperature per book

The series varies overall temperature by book to keep the season from settling into one texture.

- **Book One (The Last Account):** cold-leaning. Register A dominates the middle third. Register B is present but tender, because the fracture at the end requires the family to be real first. Register C carries the Weaver meetings and the gala.
- Later-book temperature guidance lives in `canon/02_SERIES_BIBLE.md` under Season Voice.

## Chapter-level register declaration

Every chapter draft header should declare its dominant register and any planned transitions:

```
Register: A (dominant), C (opening two pages)
```

The mandatory continuity workflow verifies register discipline as part of the voice audit.
