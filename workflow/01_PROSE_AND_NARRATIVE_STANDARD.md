# Prose and Narrative Standard

## Non-negotiable quality rule

No novel may read as though it was written, drafted, revised, or edited by AI.

Anything on the AI-prone list below must be flagged for human review before the chapter is accepted, even when the writer believes the usage is earned.

## Viewpoint and tense

- Close third person, past tense.
- One viewpoint per scene.
- Jack is primary viewpoint.
- Secondary viewpoints are limited and purposeful: Avery, Nina, Marcus, Anton, Reese, Hattie, Addison, Stephen, and Chelsea where mapped.
- No head-hopping.
- The narration may not reveal facts the viewpoint character does not know.
- Each viewpoint has a locked voice card in `workflow/05_POV_VOICE_CARDS.md`. A blind reader should identify the POV within two paragraphs.

## Register

Every scene operates in one of three registers defined in `workflow/04_VOICE_REGISTER_STANDARD.md`:

- **A — Operational** (Heat): access, entry, surveillance, danger.
- **B — Domestic/Crew** (Leverage): found-family scenes.
- **C — Elite/Adversarial** (White Collar/Suits): rooms where credibility is currency.

The register determines paragraph shape, sentence length, dialogue density, and interior-monologue budget. Register selection is not optional; it is the first drafting decision after POV.

## Tone

- Adult.
- Clean and controlled.
- Urbane when the setting warrants it.
- Credible under professional scrutiny.
- Cinematic through geography and consequence, not screenplay shorthand.
- Dry humor grounded in character.
- Emotion carried through behavior, choice, and withheld speech more often than declaration.

## Numeric and specificity default

When the viewpoint character would notice a measurable thing, name it.

- Times of day: `9:43 p.m.`, not "a little before ten."
- Dates: relative until the master calendar is locked; then exact.
- Durations: exact when the character is tracking them under pressure; approximate when they are not.
- Money: to the actual figure the character knows.
- Distances: feet, yards, blocks. Never "a short walk" when the character has walked it before.
- Institutions, vendors, addresses, streets: named specifically. No "an expensive restaurant."

Vague temporal or spatial phrasing is permitted only when the character genuinely does not know or is not paying attention.

## Sentence architecture

- Short simple declaratives dominate operational and adversarial prose.
- Sentence lengths must vary. No two adjacent sentences within one word of each other unless the repetition is deliberate.
- Em dashes are restricted. Use sparingly for a genuine interrupted thought or a sharp aside. Never as a stylish substitute for a comma, colon, or period. A page with more than two em dashes is flagged.
- Semicolons are restricted. Prefer a period. A page with more than two semicolons is flagged.
- Nested subordinate clauses are restricted. Prefer breaking into two sentences.
- No "literary run-on" cadence. The prose should not sound like the narrator is thinking aloud.

## Paragraphing

- Beat-paragraphs (a single short sentence set alone) are permitted and expected in Register A. They pace the reader through a professional under load.
- Beat-paragraphs are restricted in Register B and Register C, where longer paragraphs carry warmth or civility.
- Fragments *within* paragraphs (sentence-level fragments used for manufactured intensity) remain restricted in all registers.

## Interior monologue budget

- Interior thought is minimal in Register A, moderate in Register B, and lean-but-present in Register C.
- No on-the-page epiphany narration. The reader should watch the character realize things through action, gesture, and physical detail, not through interpretive interior sentences.
- Prohibited pattern: "For a moment he considered that…" / "He understood, then, that…" / "It occurred to him that…" and their variants.
- Prohibited pattern: naming an emotion the scene has just demonstrated.

## Dialogue

- Imperfect, interruptible, human.
- Cover dialogue in operational scenes must sound like the actual routine it imitates, not like exposition.
- Crew dialogue may use untranslated in-jokes and shorthand. Trust the reader.
- Elite dialogue is precise, cordial, subtext-loaded, and quotable. Charm is earned by carrying leverage or character, not by filling space.
- No mirrored exchanges (A says X, B says a symmetrical Y).
- No character speaking in complete paragraphs unless the scene demands a monologue and the character would deliver one.

## Distributed charm

Charm is distributed across the crew, not concentrated in Jack. Jack is devastating in Register C rooms and quieter in Register B. Nina, Reese, and Anton each carry warmth and humor in their own register-specific way. See `workflow/04_VOICE_REGISTER_STANDARD.md` for the doctrine.

## Operations

- Establish geography before action depends on it.
- Show the role of each specialist.
- Make access, surveillance, communications, timing, and exit constraints legible.
- Plant fair clues before any reveal. No information withheld from the reader that the POV character would know.
- Let plans fail for specific causal reasons.
- Avoid magic hacking, impossible physical recovery, and unexplained access.
- Injuries persist.
- The crew succeeds collectively, not because everyone else becomes incompetent.

## Violence

- Violence is dangerous, fast, and consequential.
- Jack and the crew prefer control, misdirection, escape, and disabling force.
- Killing is last-resort protection of life and carries emotional and legal consequences.

## Chapter openings

- Establish POV, register, location, and relative time within the first three paragraphs.
- Do not open with weather-as-mood.
- Do not open with a character waking up unless a chapter genuinely turns on the waking.

## Chapter endings

- End on a changed fact, decision, danger, cost, or relationship state.
- Do not force an aphorism.
- Do not force a symmetrical closing line that mirrors the opening.
- Confrontational closings should be two-beat when possible: the cover fails, then the line lands.

## AI-prone patterns — flag for review

Any of the following requires a review flag in the draft (inline `[AI-FLAG: reason]`) and a resolution before acceptance:

- Em dash used for stylistic emphasis rather than genuine interruption.
- More than two em dashes on a page.
- More than two semicolons on a page.
- Tricolon rhythm ("X, Y, and Z" with three items of similar weight and grammar).
- "Not this, but that" constructions.
- "The kind of X that Y" constructions.
- Synthetic symmetry between paired sentences or paragraphs.
- Adjacent sentences within one word of the same length.
- Repeated sentence openings across a passage.
- Uniformly polished dialogue.
- Generic emotional labels ("he felt a wave of fear").
- Summary-heavy scenes where dramatization was possible.
- Overexplaining motives the scene already demonstrates.
- Repeating the same insight in narration, dialogue, and interior thought.
- Artificially profound closing lines on every scene.
- Constant micro-cliffhangers.
- Excessive fragments used as manufactured intensity.
- Generic competence language without concrete physical or institutional detail.
- Weather-as-mood openings or closings.
- "For a moment…" / "He understood, then, that…" interior epiphany constructions.
- Naming an emotion the scene has just shown.
- Turning Jack into a narrator who announces "patterns."

## Use

- Natural sentence and paragraph variation.
- Specific sensory and professional detail selected by the viewpoint character.
- Subtext.
- Imperfect, interruptible human dialogue.
- Silence and omission where people would not explain themselves.
- Concrete cause and effect.
- Uneven emotional expression appropriate to character.
- Restraint.
