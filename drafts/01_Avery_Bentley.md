# Chapter 1 — Avery Bentley

**Draft:** v1
**POV:** Jack Mallory
**Register:** C (dominant); brief B beat at close
**Target:** ~2,300 words
**Status:** Chat draft for review

---

Jack arrived at the office at 9:15 and had thirty minutes to think about what he was going to allow.

Marley had called him on a Friday afternoon on the private line, using the phrasing Marley used only when the referral was not for money. A widow. A friend of the family. The husband had been Stephen Bentley, controller at Weaver, Newman & Gaines, dead by carbon monoxide seven weeks ago, ruled a suicide, buried, mourned, quietly rearranged in the way widows were rearranged. The firm was accusing him post-mortem of stealing eighteen million dollars from a client trust. The widow did not believe it. Marley had said she was not sentimental about her husband. She was sentimental about the truth.

Jack had said he would meet her once.

He had asked for nothing else. Marley had said her name — Avery Bentley — and one more thing.

"Jack. She is not what you think she is."

He had allowed that to be all.

At 9:45 he set the kettle on the low burner and set out two cups. He set the second cup on Avery's side of the desk because he wanted the small courtesy of the setting to be there before she was.

The door bell sounded at 9:58.

He went to it himself. He had no receptionist. He preferred to open the door for a first meeting because a great deal was decided in the first six seconds of a client's presence and he wanted to be the one deciding it.

Avery Bentley was smaller than he had expected and older than her photograph in the funeral notice. She wore a coat that was too warm for the room and did not take it off in the entry. She looked at him once, briefly, and did not smile. She said her name at the door because she was the kind of person who said her name at a door even when they both knew it.

"Mrs. Bentley."

"Avery, please."

"Come in."

She followed him down the short hall to the office. She looked at the books along the way, not the frames on the walls, and he filed that and did not name it.

She sat in the second chair without waiting for him to indicate it.

He offered tea. She accepted. He poured.

She did not drink it.

"Marley told you the outline," she said.

"She did."

"You have already decided some of what you think."

"I have decided to listen to you."

She considered him a moment across the desk and then looked away, and the looking away was not evasion. It was the small permission she was giving herself before she began.

"Seven weeks ago on a Thursday morning I woke up at 4:40 because I had not heard Stephen come home. I called his phone twice. It went to voicemail twice. At 5:13 a man from the firm called me from the office of the general counsel. He said Stephen had been found in his car in the WNG garage. He said the officers who responded believed it was a suicide. He said the firm would send someone to sit with me until my sister could come."

She stopped.

"He sent Rylan Adams."

Jack made no expression.

"Do you know who that is."

"I know the name."

"Rylan Adams sat in my living room for an hour and forty minutes. He asked me nine questions about my husband and none about how I was. When my sister arrived he stood up, gave her the number he had already written on the back of a business card, and left."

"What were the nine questions."

Avery looked at him. This was the first thing he had said that had asked her to remember specifically. She took a breath and then she took another.

"Whether Stephen had seemed different in the last month. Whether he had been keeping unusual hours. Whether he had spoken to me about any client matters. Whether he had a private laptop or phone I might not know about. Whether I had noticed him receiving mail at home from anyone at the firm. Whether he had any friends at the firm we saw socially. Whether there were financial pressures I had not been told about. Whether Stephen had been drinking. Whether Stephen had a therapist."

"In what order."

"In that order."

"You wrote them down."

"After he left. I wrote them down before I let my sister take my phone."

Jack made a small nod that was not agreement.

She had prepared this account. He wanted to know how many times she had prepared it and to whom she had prepared it. He asked neither. She would tell him or she would not.

"The firm sent flowers on Friday," she said. "A card on Monday. On Tuesday of the second week they sent me a letter accusing Stephen of stealing eighteen million dollars from a client trust between June and August."

"Signed."

"By Tomas Weaver."

"Do you have it."

"At home."

"Not with you today."

"I did not think you would need it today."

"You were right."

She permitted herself, for the first time, something at the edge of a smile. It stayed at the edge and did not move.

She reached into her bag and set three things on the desk between them.

The first was a small notebook, black, unlined, with a red elastic strap. The second was a photograph, four by six, printed on ordinary paper and folded once. The third was a small padded envelope with something in it.

"Stephen kept the notebook in the bottom drawer of his desk at home. Under a folder of tax returns from the year my mother died. The photograph was inside it. The key I found last week."

"Where."

"In the lining of his briefcase. There is a split at the bottom that has been there for at least a year and that we had been meaning to have repaired. The key had worked its way loose enough that when I picked the case up on Tuesday it moved against my hand."

Jack looked at the case-shaped absence beside her chair and understood that she had not brought the case. He was glad she had not.

"I would like to hear what he said."

"On the phone."

"On the voicemail."

She took out her phone. She unlocked it. She set it on the desk between them and pressed the button and turned the volume up.

The message was seven seconds long.

*Hey. Sorry. I'm going to be later than I said. There's an issue with the account I want to close before the morning. Don't wait up. I love you.*

Jack waited a moment and asked her to play it again.

She played it again.

He listened.

He had listened to a great many voicemails from dead men in his working life. The ones that mattered rarely mattered for what the widow heard on the first three listenings.

"He said *the* account."

"Yes."

"Not an account."

She looked at him. She had not heard the difference until he said it. He watched her hear it now.

"What does that mean."

"It means he was not calling it a client matter. It means he was calling it a thing he and you both knew about. Except that you did not know about it."

"No."

"Which means he was calling it a thing he had been carrying alone."

She held very still.

He picked up the notebook and did not open it. He picked up the photograph and unfolded it and looked at it for a long time. It was a phone-camera image of what appeared to be the front of a piece of old office furniture. Steel doors. A brass keyhole. He did not recognize it.

He set it down.

He picked up the envelope and worked it open and let the key fall into his palm.

It was small. The bit was thicker than a house key. The brass had gone the color of a penny that had been through a fire. The teeth were uneven. Along one edge someone had, at some point, filed a small notch that Jack thought was probably meant to be a mark and not a repair.

He closed his hand around it.

He set it back in the envelope.

"Avery."

"Yes."

"I am not going to accept a case today. I am going to accept a review. A small number of days. If what you have is what I suspect, I will take it then. If it is grief carefully framed as a case, I will tell you so, I will not charge you, and I will send you to two people who can help you close what needs closing."

She looked at him for a long time.

"You are being careful."

"Yes."

"Marley said you would be."

"Marley knows my work."

"Marley said something else."

"What did she say."

"She said the people who need you have already been failed by the people who should have helped them. She said that if you agreed to see me at all it meant you had already decided to look, and that everything you would say to me afterward would be about protecting yourself and protecting me. She said not to be insulted by any of it."

Jack said nothing for a moment.

Marley had said more than he had expected her to say.

"She was not wrong."

"No."

He held her eyes.

"I will keep the notebook, the photograph, and the key for a small number of days. I will have someone look at your husband's public professional record and at what was published about his death. That work will be done in ways that will not put you in front of anyone. I will need to speak with you again, probably twice, in this office, at times we will set together. I will not send anyone to your house. If someone appears at your house who says he is from me, do not open the door."

"Understood."

"There is one thing I would like you to do."

"Yes."

"When you go home this morning, you will want to play that voicemail again. Do not."

She did not answer.

"You have listened to it enough. Every time you play it, the phone records a small thing about the listening. Anyone who eventually has reason to review your device will see the pattern of the listenings. Right now, the pattern says one thing. If you play it again this morning, it will begin to say a different thing. Leave it as it is."

She nodded once, small, and put the phone away.

She stood.

He walked her to the door.

At the door she turned back and looked at him the way she had looked at him in the entry when she arrived.

"Mr. Mallory."

"Jack."

"Jack. Whatever he did that night. He did not steal eighteen million dollars from anyone."

"I know."

"You don't yet."

"I know enough."

She did not smile.

She left.

He closed the door and stood in the entry for a moment with his hand still on the latch.

He went back to the office and stood at the desk and looked at the three objects and the two undrunk cups of tea.

He picked up his phone and did not dial Marley.

He set it down.

The account was not money.
