# Chapter 1 — Avery Bentley

**Draft:** v5 (fresh from outline)
**POV:** Jack Mallory, close third
**Register:** C dominant
**Status:** Fresh literary rewrite, no reference to prior drafts

---

Marley was on the porch when Jack came around the drive, and she raised her hand at him but did not come down the steps.

He parked at the far end of the row where a Cadillac and two Volvos and a small green MG had gathered in the way old-money cars gathered on a Tuesday morning in October, and he walked back along the gravel with his coat over his arm and up the porch steps to where Marley was standing with a cigarette she was not smoking held loosely between two fingers.

"You quit."

"I quit last winter. I unquit for this morning."

"That bad."

"That kind of morning."

She held the cigarette out to him. He shook his head. She stubbed it out on the porch rail and put the filter in her coat pocket, because Marley Nielsen was too well brought up to leave a filter on a porch rail even at a club where she was a member in the fifth generation.

"She is inside. I gave her the little room off the north porch. It has a door. The girl brought her coffee at nine and I told her not to come back."

"How long has she been here."

"Since a little after nine."

"And what has she done in the little room off the north porch for an hour."

"She has stood at the window. The light there is lovely in October, and she asked me if she could stand at the window instead of sit down, which is a question a woman like her only asks when she needs to have asked something."

Jack nodded and did not answer.

"What am I walking into, Marley."

Marley looked at him. She was in a tweed skirt and jacket and the pearls, and she had done her mouth for him though she had not needed to do it, and he understood that she was reminding him of who she was and of what she was asking.

"A widow who has not been permitted to grieve because she has been too busy not believing what she has been told. A woman who was raised the way I was raised, which means she will not use two words where one will do. A woman who loved her husband, which is not always the case, and who is angry, which is more often the case than people admit. She has thought about you for a week. She thought about you again in her car this morning. Whatever you do in there, Jack, do not condescend to her. She has been condescended to for seven weeks by better men than you and I."

"I have never condescended to anyone in your presence."

"I know. I am mentioning it because I know how much you use silence, and silence can be a form of condescension to a woman who is already trying not to speak."

"Understood."

"Come."

She led him inside through the main dining room, which was quiet at this hour and would fill for lunch, and then along a shorter corridor to a smaller room at the far side of the porch. She opened the door for him and stood aside and did not follow him through and did not close the door hard behind him.

The room was small in the way old private rooms were small when they had been designed for four members and their guests and had never been enlarged. A round table set for two. A sideboard along one wall with a silver coffee service. And on the far wall a run of tall windows that looked out onto a stand of maples that had turned in the last week and now stood in the light like something a painter would have refused to paint on the grounds that no one would have believed him.

The woman at the window turned.

She was not the woman he had imagined on the phone with Marley the week before, and she was not the woman in the small photograph in the newspaper obituary, and Jack understood in the second before she said his name that most of what he had been told about her had been true and none of it had prepared him.

"Mr. Mallory."

"Jack, please."

"Jack. I am Avery. Thank you for coming out."

Her voice was low and even and precisely at the volume a voice had to be to carry the length of a room without filling it, and she crossed to him and offered her hand and shook his properly, and her hand was cool but not cold and steady but not stiff, and she did not let it linger and she did not withdraw it quickly.

"Marley has told me you were kind to her once."

"Marley is kind to herself and to other people, and I try not to disappoint her when I can help it."

She smiled at that, briefly and truly, and Jack made a small note that he had been given something and that Avery had wanted him to be given it.

"Would you sit."

"Please."

She gestured to the two chairs and took the one facing the door, and Jack understood without being told that she had been in that chair for the last hour because it was the chair a woman took in a room whose door she needed to be able to see, and that she had probably not thought about the choice, because she had been married for eight years to a man who paid attention to who was in a room and how they had come in, and she had married one of the habits that had come with him.

He took the chair across from her, which put his back to the door, which was the correct courtesy and the correct signal.

She poured for him.

She did not ask him first. She poured for him from the silver pot on the sideboard side of the table because it was the courtesy she had been taught, and she poured with the small competence of a woman who had poured for a thousand people whose approval she had not needed, and she set the cup in front of him with the handle at an angle his right hand would find without looking. Then she poured her second cup of the morning and set it in front of herself and wrapped her hands around it.

She did not drink.

"Marley said you would want to know what I have."

"Marley said correctly. But I would rather begin with what you have been through, if you do not mind. If it is easier to begin with the objects, we can begin with the objects. Whichever comes more naturally to you."

Avery considered him for a moment.

"Neither will come naturally."

"No. I know."

"I would rather begin at the beginning. It is how I have kept myself company for seven weeks."

"Then begin at the beginning."

She looked at the coffee for a long moment before she spoke, and Jack understood that she was calibrating not what she was going to say but the voice in which she was going to say it, and he waited.

"Seven weeks ago on a Thursday morning I opened my eyes at ten to five with the certain knowledge that Stephen had not come home in the night, and I lay in bed with that knowledge for what turned out to be eleven minutes but felt like a great deal longer, and I did every one of the small ordinary things a wife does in those minutes before she decides she has to pick up her phone. I told myself he had fallen asleep at his desk, though he had not done so in years. I told myself the clock was wrong. I told myself I would give him another twenty minutes. I told myself, briefly and shamefully, that if he was with a woman I did not want to know about it before breakfast. And then I picked up the phone, because that is what you do."

She paused. She did not lift her cup.

"He did not answer. I did not expect him to answer by the second try. I got up and I went downstairs. I do not remember making coffee, though I must have, because a cup was in my hand when the general counsel of Weaver Newman called me a little after six in the morning from a landline I had never dialed, and told me that my husband had been found in his car in the WNG garage, and that the officers who had responded had made their initial determination that he had done it to himself."

She stopped. She looked at the coffee. She looked back at Jack.

"He called it a determination. That is the word he used. My husband had been dead for six hours by the time I understood he was gone, and a lawyer on the other end of a phone was using the word *determination.* I did not fault him for it at the time. He was frightened and he was using the words he had. But I have thought about that word every day since he used it, and I do not want to think about it again this morning if I can help it."

"You do not have to."

"Thank you."

She lifted her cup and took a small mouthful of the coffee, which had gone room-warm in her hands, and she set it down and did not comment on the temperature because commenting on the temperature of a cup of coffee she had been holding for an hour was not what she was here to do.

"He said the firm would send someone to sit with me until my sister could come. I remember sitting in my kitchen holding a coffee I did not remember making and thinking that *sit with me* was a strange phrase for a man who had just used the word *determination* to have then chosen. One sits with a person who is dying. One does not sit with a woman whose husband has died several hours ago in a garage."

"They sent Rylan Adams."

"He arrived thirty-six minutes after the phone call. He was in a dark suit. He had shaved that morning."

"Yes."

She smiled a smile that had nothing at all to do with amusement.

"Marley described him to me last week and I told her I had described him to myself the same way a hundred times. She said you would understand him without my having to explain. Is that correct."

"Yes."

"He sat in my living room for the better part of two hours. He asked me nine questions about Stephen, and none about me, and by the fifth question I had understood that he was not there to sympathize. He was there to collect. He asked whether Stephen had been drinking. I said *no more than usual,* which is both truthful and useless. He asked whether Stephen had a therapist. I said *no,* which was true and would have been my answer either way. When my sister arrived he stood up like a man for whom the appointment had concluded, and he put a card on my kitchen counter with a number written on the back in a hand I did not recognize, and he left."

"You did not give him what he wanted."

"No."

"Good."

She looked at him.

"You are the first person in seven weeks who has said *good* to that. Everyone else has told me that I ought to have been kinder to him, or that I ought to have wept. I did not weep. I sat on my couch after he left and I noticed that the seat cushion beside me was still warm from him, and I noticed that I did not want to touch it. I sat there in the shape a woman sits in when she is waiting for something that is not going to come."

Jack said nothing. He wanted to say something and he chose not to, because saying anything he had not been asked for would have been the small betrayal Marley had warned him against.

Outside, on the course, a mower had started somewhere in the middle distance and now moved along the fairway in the low deliberate way mowers moved when there was no hurry to their morning. The light in the room had crept across the tablecloth by a hand's width, and a soft round of laughter carried through the closed door from the main dining room, and the smell of the coffee in the pot had begun to be replaced by the smell of the room the coffee had been sitting in for an hour, which was a smell of old varnish and cold air from beneath the porch door and the polish someone had used on the sideboard that morning.

Avery took a slower sip of her coffee than she had taken before.

"I have brought you four things. Three of them fit on the table."

"Then let us have the three."

She reached into the leather bag beside her chair.

The first was a small book. Black, cloth-bound, unlined, wrapped by a red rubber band that had gone limp with use. The second was a piece of ordinary printer paper folded once. The third was a padded mailing envelope, small enough to disappear in a coat pocket, holding something that did not weigh much.

She set them out in the order she had taken them from the bag.

"Stephen kept the book in the bottom drawer of his desk at home, under an old folder of tax returns. He knew I would not look in the tax returns from the year my mother died. I opened the book far enough last week to know that whatever he was recording he was recording in a shorthand I do not know. I put it back where I had found it and I closed the drawer. I did not want to be the person who understood it before I brought it to you."

"That was well done."

"The photograph was folded inside the front cover. It is a photograph of something I have never seen."

"And the key."

"Last week. The lining of his old briefcase has had a split along the bottom seam for at least a year, and Stephen was superstitious about that case in the small quiet way he was superstitious about certain things, and he would not have had it repaired had he lived. On Tuesday afternoon I picked the case up off the chair in his study because I had decided I could not walk past it another day, and the weight of the case shifted in my hand in a way it should not have shifted, and I opened the case and I felt along the lining and I found the key."

"Was it easy to find, once you started looking."

"No. He had pushed it beneath the base panel of the case, which is a rigid piece of board covered in leather. I would not have found it on the first search. I found it because I was looking."

Jack looked at the three objects and did not immediately reach for any of them. He looked at Avery. She was watching him. Her hands were flat on the table now on either side of her cup, and the tendons on the backs of them were working slightly, and she was allowing him to see them working.

"And the fourth thing."

"The fourth thing is on my phone. My phone is in my bag. I would like to be the one to press the button, if that is all right."

"That is more than all right."

She took the phone out. She held it in both hands for a moment without unlocking it. Then she unlocked it and set it face up on the table between them and pressed the small green icon at the top of the screen, and Stephen Bentley's voice came into the small dining room at the Country Club in Brookline for what was, Jack understood, some large number of times she had permitted it into a room since the morning it had happened.

*Hey. Sorry. I'm going to be later than I said. There's an issue with the account I want to close before the morning. Don't wait up. I love you.*

He listened.

He watched her while it played. She was looking at the phone, not at him. Her mouth had gone still, and when the recording ended she left her right hand where it was on the table beside the phone and did not lift it.

The room was very quiet.

He gave her the silence. He gave it to her for a count that was longer than he would have given most people, because most people would have moved to fill it, and Avery Bentley did not need to fill it. He understood, watching her not fill it, that he was watching a woman keep company with her husband for the small number of seconds she had left in which he was allowed to speak to her.

"I would like to hear it again, Avery. Not now. In a minute. When you are ready."

She nodded and did not look up.

"Take a moment."

She took the moment.

Then she lifted her head and looked at him with dry eyes, and Jack saw for the first time all morning something of what she must have looked like on the day she had married the man whose voice had just been in the room.

"Jack."

"Yes."

"You loved someone once."

"Yes."

"Are they still alive."

"Yes."

"Do you know where they are."

"I know exactly where they are."

"Then you do not know what I am about to ask you to help me carry."

"I know enough of it to help you carry it."

She held his eyes. Then she nodded, once, and she was ready.

He picked up the notebook first. He did not open it. He weighed it briefly in his palm, the way a person weighed something whose weight was not the point, and set it back on the table. He unfolded the photograph. It was a phone-camera image of a piece of old office furniture, green steel doors and a brass keyhole set into a small square plate, and he looked at it long enough to be certain he did not recognize what he was looking at, and long enough that Avery would understand that he was not going to pretend he did. He folded it and set it down.

He opened the padded envelope.

The key came out into his palm.

It was smaller than he had expected and warm to the touch from having been in her bag, and the brass had gone the color of a penny that had been in a drawer for the better part of a century. The teeth were uneven, and along one edge a small notch had been filed at some point, so lightly that only a person who was looking for it would find it, and it seemed to be a mark rather than a repair.

He closed his fingers around the key and held it. He set it back on the envelope.

"Would you play the message once more, Avery."

She did.

He listened for what he had come to listen for the second time, and it was there, in the small definite article Stephen had used to describe what he was going into the office to close, and in the small breath he had taken before the last three words he had ever said to his wife.

He waited a moment after the recording had ended.

"Avery."

"Yes."

"Your husband said *the* account. Not *an* account."

She looked at him, and her face changed in the way a person's face changed when a small thing they had heard a hundred times had suddenly assembled itself into meaning.

"Say that again."

"He said *the.* Which means he was not describing what he was going in to close as one client matter among many. He was describing it as a single thing that the two of you knew about. Which means, since you did not know about it, that he had been carrying it by himself."

"He should not have done that."

"No. He was trying to correct it, that morning."

"He should have corrected it in September."

"Yes."

She looked at the phone. She looked at the window. She looked back at him.

"Tell me what you would like to do."

"I would like to keep the book and the photograph and the key with me for a few days. I would like to sit with them, on my own time, and read the book properly, and let the photograph and the key tell me what they can tell me, before I decide what I think. I will come back to you at the end of the week. If what I have found is what I am starting to think it may be, we will talk together then about what happens next, and about what you would like to have happen, and about whether you would like me to continue. If I am wrong about what I think it may be, and I have been wrong before, I will tell you so honestly. There will not be a bill. And if it turns out there is nothing here that a firm like this one cannot handle, I will put you in the hands of two people in this city who can help you close whatever it is that still needs closing."

"You are being careful."

"Yes."

"Marley said you would be. She said something else. She said that if you agreed to see me it would mean you had already decided to look, and that whatever you said to me afterward would be about protecting me and about protecting yourself, and that I was not to be insulted by any of it."

"That is fair."

"Are you protecting yourself, Jack."

"A little."

"Are you protecting me."

"Yes. Rather more."

She held his eyes for a long moment.

"I have not been protected in seven weeks."

"I know."

"Thank you."

He held her eyes back.

"There is one thing I would ask you, Avery, before you go. When you get home this morning, you are going to want to play that voicemail again. I would like you to try not to."

"Why."

"Because a phone keeps a record of when it is played and how many times, and right now the shape of that record says one thing about you. Once you play it again this morning, it begins to say something else. Leave it as it is, for today. You have listened to it enough for a Tuesday."

She nodded, slowly, and put the phone away in her bag. Her hand shook a little on the small motion of sliding it in. She let him see her hand shake, and she did not apologize for it, and Jack thought that she had made a decision at some point in the last seven weeks about what she was going to permit herself to be seen doing in front of a man she had not previously met, and that she was permitting him more than she had permitted most of the people who had passed through her house that fall.

She stood.

He stood with her.

He walked her to the door of the small room. He did not follow her through. She turned before she went.

"Jack."

"Yes."

"Whatever my husband did the night he died, he did not steal eighteen million dollars from anyone. I do not require you to believe me. I would like you to know that I have said it."

"I believe you."

"You do not know yet."

"I know enough already."

She smiled again then, small and closed, and it stayed for the length of two of her breaths. Then she was through the door, and Marley closed it behind her from the other side.

He stood alone in the small room for a moment with his hand resting on the back of the chair she had been sitting in. The light had crept another hand's width across the tablecloth. The maples outside had gone from red to something closer to blood in the way October did on a Tuesday morning when the sun found its way through late. A short round of laughter carried through the door again from the main dining room.

He gathered the three objects into the inside pocket of his coat.

He walked out through the empty dining room and past the bar and did not stop to speak to Marley on his way through.

The account was not money.
