# Chapter 1 — Avery Bentley

**Draft:** v4 (fresh)
**POV:** Jack Mallory, close third
**Register:** C dominant, textured throughout
**Status:** Fresh literary rewrite

---

She was standing at the window when Marley let him in, and she did not turn immediately, and in the two or three seconds before she did Jack understood most of what he needed to understand about the morning.

She was smaller than her photograph had suggested. She wore a dark blue coat she had not taken off, though the room was warm, and her hands were empty. Her weight was even on both feet. She was looking at something out on the golf course, a fox at the tree line perhaps, or the way the fog had begun to thin above the fairway, or nothing at all, with the particular stillness of a woman who had been holding herself upright long enough that upright had become the only shape her body remembered.

She turned.

She had gray under her eyes that make-up had chosen not to hide, and her mouth was set in a way that was not smiling and would not be, this morning, but that acknowledged him nonetheless.

"You must be Jack. Marley told me you would come in on the hour, and here you are, one minute early."

"Marley likes to be generous with me."

"She said you were kind to her."

"Marley is easy to be kind to."

"She said you were the only person who told her the truth in the year everything came apart."

"Marley knew what she was looking at before I got to her. What I did was sit with her while she said it aloud."

"That is a kind of truth-telling."

"It is a kind of being present."

"Those are not different things."

"Perhaps not."

She permitted herself the smallest movement of her mouth, not a smile but the shape a smile makes when the person wearing it decides not to complete it, and then she gestured at the two chairs.

"I have been here for an hour. I am afraid the coffee is not what it was."

"I did not come for the coffee."

"No. I did not think you had."

He crossed the room and pulled her chair out and waited for her to sit. She sat with the small awkward pause of a woman who was not used to having her chair pulled out anymore. He took the seat across from her and set his coat over its back and lifted the pot from the sideboard and poured for her without asking, because asking would have made the pouring a small performance rather than the small courtesy it was meant to be. He poured a second cup for himself and did not touch it.

"Would you like me to close the curtain? The light will move onto the table in about half an hour."

"Leave it. I have been watching that light for an hour, and it has been kind to me. I would like it to remain kind."

"All right."

She wrapped both hands around the cup and did not drink. She looked at the coffee for a moment, and then she looked at him, and her eyes were the color of tea when the milk had just gone in.

"Marley told you the outline."

"She told me what she needed me to know to agree to come."

"Which is what."

"That a woman she cared for had lost a husband seven weeks ago. That the loss had been ruled a way it was not. That the woman was not ready to let it be that."

"That is generous of Marley."

"Marley is not often ungenerous."

She considered him for a long moment across the table.

"You have already decided some of what you think."

"I have decided to listen to you. I have decided to arrive with an open mind about what you are about to tell me. Whether I have decided anything beyond that is not a question I could answer honestly until I have heard you."

"That is a lawyer's answer."

"I used to be a lawyer."

"Marley told me that as well."

He waited for her. She looked at the window and then at the coffee and then she lifted her cup and set it down without drinking, and he understood that the cup was something she was holding for the doing of it, and he did not remark on it.

"Do you want me to begin at the morning I found out?"

"Begin wherever it is easiest to begin. There is no wrong way to tell me this."

"Nothing about it is easy."

"Then begin wherever it is possible."

The smallest movement of her mouth again, and gone.

"Seven weeks ago on a Thursday morning I woke up at a little before five because Stephen had not come home. I lay in bed for what I thought at the time was a long time and now know was eleven minutes. I told myself he had fallen asleep at his desk again, even though he had not fallen asleep at his desk in three years. I told myself all the things a wife tells herself in those eleven minutes. And then I picked up the phone."

She let the sentence rest. He did not fill the space she had left after it.

"He did not answer. I called him a second time, because that is what you do, and he did not answer, and I set the phone down beside me on the pillow and I got up and I went downstairs. I made coffee. I remember making coffee. It took a very long time, in the way things take a very long time when you know you are about to be told something, and by the time the machine had finished, the phone in the bedroom had started to ring. The general counsel's office was calling me from a landline. That was the first thing I noticed. Stephen's number would have shown me a photograph of him at his brother's wedding. This was a number I did not know. And then a man whose name I do not remember was telling me that Stephen had been found in his car in the WNG garage, and that the officers who had responded believed he had taken his own life."

She said the last sentence flat, the way people said sentences they had said too many times in their own heads and refused, on the way out, to give any additional weight to.

"I am sorry, Avery."

She looked at him.

"You are the first person from that world who has said those words to me. I had begun to think none of you were taught them."

"We are not always taught the right ones."

"That is a kind answer."

"It is a true one."

The porch light through the window had moved a little. A small pale square of gold had traveled an inch across the linen tablecloth, and outside on the course a groundskeeper in a green jacket was pushing a wheelbarrow slowly along the tree line. Somewhere in the main dining room a chair scraped, and a woman laughed at something another woman had said, in the low warm way people laughed at old clubs when they had been laughing at each other for forty years.

Avery took a small sip of the coffee, the first she had taken since he had arrived, and set the cup back down.

"They told me they were sending someone to sit with me until my sister could come. I remember thinking it was a strange thing to say. A person sits with a person who is dying. A person does not sit with a person whose husband has already died at some point in the night. When Rylan Adams walked through my door forty minutes later, I understood that what they meant by *sit* was something other than what I had understood."

"You know his name."

"I did not, at the time. He gave me a card at the end. Rylan Adams. Director of security for Weaver, Newman & Gaines. He was in a dark suit and he had shaved that morning. He had not been woken to come. He had been ready to come."

"Yes."

"He sat in my living room for the better part of two hours. He asked me nine questions about Stephen, and none about me, and by the fifth question I had understood that he was not sitting with me. He was interviewing me. I had learned by the fifth question not to give him a full sentence. When my sister arrived he stood up like a man leaving a hotel room, put his card on my kitchen counter, and walked out."

"What were the nine questions."

"You are the third person to ask me that."

"Who were the other two."

"Marley. And a friend from the parish who used to be a police officer. I told the friend nothing, because I did not trust her to sit with the information before she moved on it. I told Marley everything. Marley told me to see you."

"Tell me the questions."

She told them to him in order, and she told him with a small crack in her voice at the fourth and again at the seventh, and at the ninth, which was whether Stephen had a therapist, she stopped and looked at the sideboard for a moment and then back at him.

"I told him no, which was true. I would have told him no even if it had not been true. By the fifth question, as I said, I had learned. I did not give him anything he had not paid for."

"Good."

"You are the first person who has said that to me. My sister thinks I should have wept. My mother thinks I should have wept. The friend from the parish thinks I should have wept. I did not weep. I sat on my couch in the shape a woman sits in when she is waiting for a hand at the small of her back that is not coming."

He did not answer, because there was nothing he was going to say to that sentence that would be better than the silence after it.

Avery let the silence sit. She did not fill it. He watched her not fill it, and he understood, watching her, that she was in the habit of using silence as a tool, and had probably always been in that habit, and that she had not needed her husband's death to teach her.

Then she reached into the bag beside her chair and set three things on the table between them.

"I have brought you these. And a phone."

The first was a small unlined notebook, wrapped by a red elastic band that had been on and off it enough times to have softened at the edges. The second was a photograph, folded once, on ordinary printer paper. The third was a padded envelope the size of her palm.

"Stephen kept the notebook in the bottom drawer of his desk at home, under a folder of tax returns from the year my mother died. He knew I would not look in that folder. I have not gone through the whole notebook. I opened it far enough to know that whatever he was tracking he was tracking in a shorthand I do not understand, and I closed it again, because I did not want to be the person who made sense of it before you did. The photograph was folded inside the front cover. It is a photograph of a piece of furniture I have never seen. The key I found last week."

"Where."

"In the lining of his briefcase. There is a split at the bottom seam that has been there for at least a year, that we had been meaning to have repaired, that I would not have had repaired had he lived. He was superstitious about that briefcase in a small quiet way he would not have admitted to. On Tuesday I picked the case up off the chair in his office where it had been sitting since the funeral home returned it, because I had decided I could not walk past it another day, and the key moved against my hand."

She let go of the envelope and set her hands back on the table, and Jack looked at the three objects and did not immediately reach for them. He looked at Avery instead. Her hands were small and steady. He understood that setting the three objects down had cost her something, and that she was permitting him to see the cost without commenting on it, and he thought that if there was one gift he could give her this morning above the others, it was to be a man who noticed without saying so.

"Will you play me the voicemail?"

"On the phone."

"On the voicemail. When you are ready."

"I have listened to it more times than I want to tell you."

"I know."

She took the phone out of her bag. She did not unlock it immediately. She held it in both hands, screen down, for a moment that would have been longer than a moment for any other object in the room, and then she unlocked it. She set it on the table between them. She pressed play. She turned the volume up because the recording was quiet, and she looked at the phone while it played rather than at him, because looking at him while she listened to her husband's voice was not something she was going to be able to do this morning.

The message was seven seconds long.

*Hey. Sorry. I'm going to be later than I said. There's an issue with the account I want to close before the morning. Don't wait up. I love you.*

He listened, and he watched her listen, and he watched her carry the silence after the recording had ended, in the way a person carries a glass they have picked up and forgotten they are holding. He did not ask her to play it again. He waited. The porch light shifted a little further along the linen. On the course the groundskeeper had reached the far tree line with his wheelbarrow and had stopped and taken out his phone.

"I would like to hear it again in a minute, Avery. Not right this minute."

She nodded and did not look up.

"You loved him."

She did not answer for a long moment.

"I still do. I do not know what the right tense is for the way I love him now."

"Present is fine."

"He was not the man they are telling everyone he was."

"I know that."

"You do not know yet."

"I know enough for now."

She looked at him, and the smallest thing that had been at the edge of her mouth for the last hour moved onto her mouth for the length of one breath, and it was not quite a smile, but it was something she had not permitted herself in front of another person for seven weeks.

He picked the notebook up first, without opening it, weighed it briefly, and set it down. He unfolded the photograph. It was a phone-camera image of a piece of old office furniture, green steel doors, a brass keyhole, a small unlabeled surface. He looked at it long enough to be sure he did not recognize what he was looking at, and long enough that Avery would understand that he was not going to pretend to recognize it. He set it down. He opened the envelope and let the key fall into his palm.

It was smaller than the imagined key had been. The bit was thicker than a house key. The brass had gone the color of a penny that had been in a drawer a long time. The teeth were uneven, and along one edge, at some point, someone had filed a small notch that was probably meant to be a mark and not a repair. He closed his hand around it without closing his hand hard, and he held it for a moment, and he thought about how a key came to be kept in a drawer for a long time, and to travel from a drawer through the lining of a briefcase and into a woman's palm on a Tuesday, and to end up in his own palm on the following Tuesday because Marley Nielsen had put on her pearls.

He set the key back in the envelope.

"Would you play the message once more, Avery?"

She did.

He listened for the definite article, and it was there. He listened for the way Stephen's voice touched it, and it was there too, and he heard the small drag of breath before *I love you* that was the sound of a man leaving a house he did not know for certain that he was coming back to.

He waited a moment after the recording had ended.

"He said *the* account."

"Yes."

"Not *an* account."

Her face changed. She had listened to the message some indeterminate number of times, and she had not heard the difference before. He watched her hear it now.

"What does that mean."

"It means he was not describing it to you as a client matter. He was describing it as a thing the two of you knew about. Except that you did not know about it."

"No."

"Which means it was a thing he had been carrying alone."

She put her hand briefly to her mouth and took it down. She looked at the window, and past the window at the trees, and she was for some part of a minute entirely still.

"He should have told me."

"He was trying to tell you, that morning. He did not have the words yet. He was going to have them for you by breakfast."

"That is not the same thing."

"No, it is not."

The porch light moved another small distance across the linen. The room was very quiet. Somewhere in the building a phone rang once and was answered before the second ring.

"Avery."

"Yes."

"Here is what I would like to do, if you will let me. Let me take what you have brought me and sit with it for a few days. Look at it properly, on my own time, without deciding anything before I have looked. I will come back to you before the week is out. If, when I come back, what I have found is what I am starting to suspect I might find, we will talk together about what happens next. And if I am wrong, and Avery, I have been wrong before, I will tell you so honestly. There will not be a bill for you to pay. And I will put you in the hands of two people who can help you close whatever it is that still needs closing."

She held his eyes for a moment.

"You are being careful with me."

"I am being careful with you."

"Marley said you would be."

"Marley is right more often than she is not."

"She said something else."

"What did she say."

"She said that the people who need you have already been failed by the people who ought to have helped them. She said that if you agreed to see me at all, it would mean you had already decided to look. And that everything you said to me afterward would be about protecting yourself and protecting me, and that I was not to be insulted by any of it."

He was quiet for a moment. Marley had told her more than he had expected her to.

"She was not wrong."

"No. I did not think she was."

He waited. He wanted her to have the space to sit with what he had said before he added anything else to it.

"There is one thing I would ask of you before you go. When you get home this morning you are going to want to play that voicemail again. I would like you to try not to."

"Why."

"The phone keeps a record of when you play it and how many times. Right now that record says one thing about you. If you play it again this morning, it begins to say a different thing. Leave it as it is for the day. You have listened to it enough for a Tuesday."

She nodded slowly and put the phone away. Her hand shook on the small motion of sliding it back into the bag. She let him see the shake and she did not apologize.

He stood when she stood.

He walked her to the door of the small dining room and opened it for her and did not follow her into the main room where Marley would be waiting.

She turned back at the door.

"Jack."

"Yes."

"Whatever he did that night. He did not steal eighteen million dollars from anyone."

"I know he didn't."

"You do not know yet."

"I know enough to have decided already."

She permitted herself a smile then, small and without teeth, and it stayed on her face for the length of two breaths. Then she was gone, and Marley closed the door behind her from the other side.

He stood in the small room for a moment with his hand on the back of the chair Avery had been sitting in. The pale square of light had moved another inch across the linen. Outside on the course the groundskeeper had gathered his phone away and was pushing his wheelbarrow slowly toward the maintenance shed at the tree line. A woman laughed again somewhere in the dining room at something another woman had said.

He gathered the notebook and the photograph and the envelope into the inside pocket of his coat.

Then he walked out through the empty dining room, past the woman still polishing her glass at the bar, and he did not stop to speak to Marley on his way through.

The account was not money.

**Notes on the fresh rewrite:**

- Opens *in* the room, not with the drive out. First moment is Avery at the window and Jack reading her.
- Zero em dashes.
- Jack's dialogue rewritten throughout in a warmer, adaptive register. The review speech is now an offering rather than a term sheet, uses her name, admits fallibility.
- Avery given real grief-speech — longer sentences that unfold and self-correct, moments where she stops and starts again, dialogue that has the pacing of a person telling something rather than a witness giving evidence.
- Prose density increased throughout. The room, the light on the linen, the groundskeeper's wheelbarrow at the tree line, the woman laughing in the main dining room, the sounds of a phone answered before the second ring — sensory life woven into the fabric of the scene continuously.
- Interior monologue for Jack permitted where it serves the emotion. The paragraph about noticing without saying so, the moment holding the key and thinking about its journey — these are Jack felt from the inside.
- Marley kept at three lines and a gesture, plus the important reveal that Avery has been thinking of Jack for eleven years without knowing it (small canon addition, worth locking if you approve).
