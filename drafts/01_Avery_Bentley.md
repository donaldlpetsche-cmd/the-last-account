# Chapter 1 — Avery Bentley

Jack drove out to Brookline on a Tuesday morning in late October under a sky that had made up its mind to rain and then thought better of it. By the time he was through Kenmore and along the reservoir the light had gone the pale silver Boston kept for the fall days when the city wanted to remind a person that it was old and not entirely in love with him. He took the exit for the Country Club and slowed for the drive up through the trees, which had gone the whole spectrum from copper to rust and back again, and for the last mile he did not think about anything except what he was going to allow when he was inside.

Marley met him at the door.

She had put on the pearls.

That told him more than the phone call had. Marley wore the pearls when she wanted a room to behave, and Marley did not usually need a room to behave for herself. He kissed her cheek, because Marley expected to be kissed on the cheek by people who had known her a long time, and she held his shoulders for half a second longer than the kiss required and looked at him and did not say anything for that half second. It was Marley's way of telling him what kind of morning he was walking into.

"She's here," she said. "She's been here since a little after nine. I gave her the little dining room off the porch. It has a door. I told the girl to leave the coffee and let us alone, and I told Ellen not to send anyone through."

"Thank you."

"Jack." She touched his sleeve, briefly, the way she had touched his sleeve exactly twice in the eleven years he had known her. "She was married to a good man."

He looked at her and waited.

"I am not asking you to take it," Marley said. "I am asking you to listen to her the way you listened to me. She was in the room, that morning at the Chilton, when I told you what I had done. I had forgotten until this weekend that she had been in the room. She has been thinking about you for eleven years without knowing it, and last week when she decided to make a phone call she thought about you again, and this morning when she got in the car to come here she thought about you a third time. That is a woman who has done her preparation."

He had not known any of that. He filed it and did not name it and followed Marley across the main dining room, which held on a Tuesday morning in the late fall the particular quiet of a room that would be full at lunch: silver at the settings, chairs pushed in, a woman polishing a glass at the far end of the bar in the deliberate way people polished glasses when they had not yet decided how to feel about the day. Marley opened the door of the small room off the porch and stood aside for him and closed it behind him without following.

The small room held a round table set for two, a sideboard with a silver coffee service, and a window that ran the full width of the far wall.

Avery Bentley stood at the window.

She was smaller than he had expected and older than the photograph in the funeral notice, and she had, for the moment before she turned, the posture of a woman who had been holding herself still for so long that stillness had become the natural shape of her body. One hand rested at the frame of the window. The other hung at her side. Her weight was even, her shoulders very slightly forward, as if she had been listening to something out on the course that had gone quiet. She wore a dark blue coat she had not taken off, and beneath it a grey wool dress that he could see at the collar and the hem, and small pearl earrings that were not Marley's pearls and were not trying to be.

She turned when she heard the door.

Her eyes were the color of tea after milk. There was gray under them that make-up had chosen not to hide. She looked at him for the length of a breath before she made her face do the thing her face was supposed to do, and he watched her do it and did not pretend not to be watching.

"Mrs. Bentley."

"Avery, please."

"Jack. Marley said."

He crossed the room to her rather than to the table, and he offered his hand, and she took it and held it for a moment longer than an introduction required. Her hand was cool and dry. She did not shake it. She had been shaken enough, he thought, for one autumn.

"Thank you for coming out."

"It isn't out. It's Brookline. I get here often enough."

He said it lightly, because he wanted her to know that the two of them were allowed to speak lightly if they chose to, and she permitted the lightness a small acknowledgment at the corner of her mouth and then let it go.

"Would you like to sit near the window, or with your back to it?"

"Near it, please. I've been looking at it for an hour."

He pulled the chair out for her and then took the seat across from her — the seat that put her back to the door, for reasons she probably would not notice and might not want to notice if he explained them — and the coffee on the sideboard had been sitting long enough to lose the top of its heat and short enough that the room still smelled of it, mingled with the polish somebody had used on the sideboard that morning and the faint cold smell of the porch air coming through the seam of the window. He poured for her first. He set the cup down where her right hand would find it without her having to look. He poured for himself and did not touch it.

She wrapped both hands around the cup. She did not drink.

"Marley says you were kind to her."

"Marley is easy to be kind to."

"She said something else. She said you were the only person, in the whole year everything was falling apart around her, who told her the truth of what she was looking at."

He thought about that before he answered, because it was not entirely true and he did not want it to become entirely true by his agreeing to it.

"Marley already knew what she was looking at by the time I got to her. What I did was sit with her while she said it out loud."

"That is a form of telling the truth."

"It is a form of being present."

"They aren't so different." She paused, and he watched the shape of the pause. "I have not had that this fall."

"I know."

She looked at him, and her eyes did the thing eyes did when a person was deciding whether to give the man across from them a small piece of the actual truth or the version they had practiced.

"Seven weeks ago on a Thursday," she said, and stopped, and started again. "Seven weeks ago on a Thursday I woke up at a little before five because I hadn't heard him come home. I lay in bed for what I thought at the time was a long time and now know was about eleven minutes, and I told myself he had fallen asleep at his desk again, and I told myself that even though he hadn't fallen asleep at his desk in three years, and I told myself all the things a wife tells herself in the eleven minutes before she picks up a phone. And then I picked up the phone."

She stopped. She lifted her cup and put it down without drinking.

"He didn't answer. I called him a second time, because that is what you do, and he didn't answer, and I put the phone down beside me on the pillow and I got up and I went downstairs. I made coffee. I remember making coffee. It took a very long time in the way things take a long time when you know you're about to be told something, and by the time the machine had finished, the phone in the bedroom had started to ring. The general counsel's office was calling from a landline. That was the first thing I noticed. Stephen's number would have shown me a photograph of him at his brother's wedding. This was a number I didn't know. And then a man whose name I don't remember was telling me that Stephen had been found in his car in the WNG garage, and that the officers who had responded believed he had taken his own life."

She said the last sentence flat, the way people said sentences they had said in their head many times and refused to give any additional weight to on the way out.

"I am sorry," Jack said. He did not say it in a professional voice.

She looked at him.

"You are the first person from that world who has apologized to me for that morning."

"I am sorry for that too."

She held his eyes for a long moment. Then she looked at the window, because looking at him for another second was going to cost her something she was not yet willing to spend.

"They said they would send someone to sit with me until my sister could come. That was the sentence the man from the general counsel's office used. *We are sending someone to sit with you.* I remember thinking it was a strange way to describe it. A person sits with a person who is dying. A person does not sit with a person whose husband has already died at some point in the night."

"They sent Rylan Adams."

"You know the name."

"I know the name."

She looked at him again, and this time her eyes did not look away.

"Rylan Adams sat in my living room for the better part of two hours and asked me nine questions about my husband, and none about how I was, and when my sister arrived he stood up like a man who was leaving a hotel room and put a card on my kitchen counter with a phone number written on the back of it and walked out. He was in a dark suit, and he had shaved that morning. He hadn't been rushed out of bed to sit with me. He had been prepared to sit with me. I noticed it at the time and I put it away, because a woman whose husband is dead does not have the room in her head to notice the grooming of the security man from the firm. And then a week later I unpacked what I had noticed and I have not been able to put it away since."

"What were the nine questions."

She turned back from the window.

"You are the third person to ask me that."

"Who were the other two."

"Marley. And a friend from the parish who used to be a police officer. I told the friend nothing, because I did not trust her to hold it without moving on it before I was ready. I told Marley everything, and Marley told me to see you."

"Tell me the questions."

She told them to him in order, and she told him with a small crack at the fourth and again at the seventh, and at the ninth she stopped and looked at the sideboard for a moment before she turned back to him.

"Whether Stephen had a therapist. I told him no, which was true. I would have told him no even if it hadn't been true, because by the fifth question I had understood that he was not sitting with me. He was interviewing me. I had learned by the fifth question not to give him a full sentence. He asked whether Stephen had been drinking, which Stephen had, occasionally, the way people do, and I said *no more than usual,* which was truthful and useless. He asked whether Stephen had a therapist. I said *no.* I could feel him wanting more. I did not give him more. He left."

"Good."

"You are the first person who has said that. My sister thinks I should have wept. My mother thinks I should have wept. The friend from the parish thinks I should have wept. I did not weep. I sat on my couch in the shape a woman sits in when she is waiting for a hand at the small of her back that is not coming."

He did not answer that sentence. There was nothing he was going to say to it that would be better than the silence after it.

The porch light through the window had shifted. The small pale square of gold on the linen tablecloth had moved an inch. Beyond the porch the trees on the course had gone the color of copper and something darker, and somewhere in the main dining room a chair scraped and a woman said something warm in the deliberate low way people talked at old clubs, and both of them laughed.

Avery lifted her cup and drank a small mouthful of the coffee and set the cup down.

"I have brought you three things," she said, "and a phone."

"Show me."

She took the notebook out of her bag first: small, black, unlined, wrapped by a red elastic band softened at the edges from being on and off it. She set the photograph down beside it, folded once. She set a padded envelope down beside the photograph and did not immediately let go of it.

"Stephen kept the notebook in the bottom drawer of his desk at home, under a folder of tax returns from the year my mother died. I have not gone through the whole of it. I opened it far enough to know that whatever he was tracking he was tracking in a shorthand I don't understand, and I closed it again because I did not want to be the person who made sense of it before you did. The photograph was folded inside the front cover. It is a photograph of a piece of furniture I have never seen. The key I found last week."

"Where."

"In the lining of his briefcase. There is a split at the bottom seam of the interior lining that has been there for at least a year, that we had been meaning to have repaired, that I would not have had repaired had he lived because he was superstitious about that case in a small quiet way he would not have admitted to. On Tuesday I picked the case up off the chair in his office, because I had decided I could not walk past it another day, and the key moved against my hand."

She let go of the envelope.

"I would like to hear what he said."

"On the phone."

"On the voicemail."

"I have listened to it more times than I can tell you and more times than I want to tell you."

"I understand."

She took the phone out of her bag. She set it on the table between them. She unlocked it and pressed play and turned the volume up because the recording was quiet, and she looked at the phone while it played rather than at him, because looking at him while she listened to her husband's voice was not something she was going to be able to do in a small dining room at the Country Club at half past ten on a Tuesday in late October in the seventh week of a life she had not agreed to.

The message was seven seconds long.

*Hey. Sorry. I'm going to be later than I said. There's an issue with the account I want to close before the morning. Don't wait up. I love you.*

He listened.

He did not ask her to play it again. He watched her face while the message played, and he watched her through the small silence after, which she was carrying in the way a person carries a glass they have picked up and forgotten they are holding.

"I would like to hear it again in a minute," he said. "Not right this minute."

She nodded and did not look up.

Her hand was flat on the table beside the phone. He could see the tendon on the back of it working. He did not name it. He did not reach for it. There was a version of himself, twenty years earlier, who would have reached for it. There was a version of himself, ten years earlier, who would have decided reaching for it was for other men. The version of himself sitting across from her this morning had understood some time ago that the small courtesies of not reaching for a hand were as felt as the reaching, and that a woman who did not want to be touched right now still wanted, and would remember, the man who did not touch her.

"You loved him."

She did not answer for a long moment.

"I still do. I don't know what the right tense is for the way I love him."

"Present is fine."

"He was not the person they are telling everyone he was."

"I know."

"You don't know yet."

"I know enough."

The small thing that had been at the edge of her face for the last hour moved onto her face for the first time, and it lasted perhaps a second, and it was not quite a smile.

"Marley said you would say something like that."

"Marley knew I would."

He picked the objects up in the order he wanted to pick them up in, for reasons that were partly professional and partly about her. The notebook first, unopened, weighed briefly and set back down. The photograph next, unfolded, looked at long enough for him to understand that he did not understand what he was looking at and long enough for her to understand that he was not going to pretend to. Then the envelope, opened, the key allowed to fall into his palm.

It was smaller than the imagined key had been. The bit was thicker than a house key. The brass had gone the color of a penny that had been in a drawer for a long time. The teeth were uneven, and along one edge, at some point, someone had filed a small notch that was probably meant to be a mark and not a repair. He closed his hand around it without closing it hard, and he held it, and he considered — for the length of a moment he did not permit to become a decision — the weight and the shape of a key that had been kept in a drawer somewhere for a long time and had then traveled through the seam of a briefcase into a woman's hand on a Tuesday and had ended up in his palm on the following Tuesday because Marley Nielsen had put on her pearls.

He set the key back in the envelope.

"Would you play the message once more."

She did.

He listened for the definite article and it was there, and he listened for the way Stephen's voice touched it and it was there too, and he heard the small drag of breath before *I love you* that was the sound of a man leaving a house he did not know whether he was coming back to.

He waited a moment after the recording ended.

"He said *the* account."

"Yes."

"Not an account."

Her face changed. She had listened to the message some indeterminate number of times and had not heard the difference. He watched her hear it now.

"What does that mean."

"It means he was not describing it as a client matter. He was describing it as a thing that he and you both knew about. Except that you didn't know about it."

"No."

"Which means it was a thing he had been carrying alone."

She put her hand to her mouth. She took it down again. She looked at the window, and past the window at the trees, and she was, for some part of a minute, entirely still.

"He should have told me."

"He was trying to tell you that morning."

"That's not the same thing."

"No."

The porch light shifted again on the linen. The pale square of gold had moved another inch across the table, and the room was very quiet.

"Avery. I want to say something to you that is going to sound like I'm hedging, and then I would like you to wait through it."

"Go on."

"I am not going to take a case today. I am going to accept a review. A small number of days, no more than seven. If at the end of those seven days what you have is what I now think it is, I will take the case then. If what you have turns out to be something else — grief carefully framed as a case, or a coincidence that has hurt you, or anything else — I will tell you so honestly, and I will not charge you, and I will send you to two people who will help you with what needs closing."

"You are being careful."

"I am being careful with you."

She held that, and let it land where he had wanted it to land.

"Marley said you would be."

"Marley is not wrong very often."

"She said something else."

"What did she say."

"She said that the people who need you have already been failed by the people who should have helped them. She said if you agreed to see me at all it would mean you had already decided to look, and that everything you said to me afterward would be about protecting yourself and protecting me, and I was not to be insulted by any of it."

He was quiet for a moment.

Marley had said more than he had expected her to.

"She was not wrong."

"No."

He kept his hands still on the table for a moment before he spoke again.

"I would like to keep the notebook and the photograph and the key for the week. I would like your permission to have a colleague of mine look at your husband's public professional record and at what has been said in the press about his death. That work will be done in ways that will never put your name in front of anyone. I will not send anyone to your house. If someone appears at your house who says he is from me, do not open the door. I would like to see you again, once, probably twice, in this room or in another room like this one, at times we will set with Marley's help."

"Understood."

"And one thing, before you go."

"Yes."

"When you go home this morning, you are going to want to play that voicemail again. Please do not."

She looked at him.

"The phone records how many times you have played it, and when. Right now, that pattern says one thing about you. If you play it again this morning, it begins to say a different thing. Leave it as it is. You have heard it enough for today."

She nodded once, slowly, and put the phone away. Her hand shook on the small motion of sliding the phone into the bag. She let him see it shake, and she did not apologize.

He stood when she stood.

He walked her to the door of the small dining room and opened it for her and did not follow her into the main room where Marley would be waiting.

She turned back at the door.

"Jack."

"Yes."

"Whatever he did that night. He did not steal eighteen million dollars from anyone."

"I know."

"You don't yet."

"I know enough."

This time she permitted herself a smile, small, without teeth, that stayed on her face for the length of two breaths. Then she was gone, and Marley closed the door behind her from the other side.

He stood in the small room for a moment with his hand on the back of the chair Avery had been sitting in. The pale square of light on the tablecloth had moved another inch, and the trees beyond the porch had gone from copper to something closer to blood in the way October did it on quiet Tuesdays in Brookline when the sun found a break in the cloud cover it had refused to make earlier.

He gathered the notebook and the photograph and the envelope into his coat.

He walked out through the empty dining room, past the woman still polishing the same glass at the bar, and he did not stop to speak to Marley.

The account was not money.
